A Deaf patient at an Indian government hospital usually cannot talk to the doctor. There are very few sign language interpreters, and a district OPD will almost never have one. So a relative translates a medical conversation they do not understand themselves, or the two sides pass notes in a language the patient may not read, or the patient just nods. Symptoms, dosage, allergies and follow-up all get lost in that.

Setu works in both directions. The patient signs and the doctor hears it spoken in their own language. The doctor speaks or types and the patient sees it signed back by an animated signer. Underneath both is a board of clinical phrases in six Indian languages for the things that have to be exact, like "where does it hurt" and "are you allergic to any medicine".

All of it runs in a browser tab on the laptop or phone the clinic already has. No server, no API key, no bill per use, and the video never leaves the device. The model is 2 MB and caches on the first visit.

That is the part we care most about, because it decides whether this can actually be used.

A district hospital is not going to sign up for a monthly cloud bill so its patients can speak. Anything that needs one gets piloted, praised, and dropped when the grant ends. Something that costs nothing to run can just be handed over.

Privacy comes from the design, not from a policy document. The patient's face and complaint are never transmitted, so there is no retention rule to audit and nothing to leak.

It also works when the internet does not, which in the places short of interpreters is often.

We split the system into layers because different parts of a consultation need different guarantees.

The phrase board is exact. It is written text, translated once and shipped with the app, and it says the same right thing every time. No model is involved. This is the main surface, and it is what a doctor should use when getting it wrong is not acceptable.

Recognition is the shortcut. A small neural network reads a clinical vocabulary from the camera and shows the doctor a short list of what it thinks was signed. Tested on a signer it has never seen, and averaged over five separate training runs, it gets the sign right first about 74% of the time and has it somewhere in the top five about 95% of the time. We built the interface around the second number. A list of five that is nearly always right is more useful in a consultation than one answer that is occasionally confidently wrong.

Past that vocabulary, another 200 words are matched against a single example of each sign taken from published Indian Sign Language dictionaries. This turned out to matter a lot. Words a patient actually needs, like bleeding, swelling, cough and most body parts, exist in those dictionaries as exactly one recording. That is nowhere near enough to train on, but it is enough to compare against.

We also spent real effort on making it shut up. A classifier handed something it has never seen does not fail cleanly, it picks whichever class is closest and reports high confidence. So Setu checks both hands were actually visible before it speaks, and its confidence numbers are calibrated so 80% roughly means 80%. In a hospital, knowing when to say nothing is worth more than always having an answer.

Who it is for: Deaf and hard of hearing patients, who can describe a symptom without bringing someone to translate. Doctors in crowded OPDs, who can take a history from a patient they otherwise could not question at all. And administrators, because there is no per-seat cost, no procurement, and nothing stopping them copying it to every terminal in a district tomorrow.

The same build works at a railway counter or a tourist desk with no changes. It is the same problem in a lighter form, and the vocabulary, phrases and sign playback carry over.

What it is not: Setu assists a conversation, it does not replace a qualified interpreter, and it should not be used for surgical consent or anything with legal weight. The trained vocabulary is small and clinical, not general. The phrase translations are machine generated and are being checked by Deaf signers. Accuracy on a live camera in a busy room will be lower than on the curated recordings we tested with, and when the model is unsure the interface falls back to the phrase board instead of guessing.

This is built and deployed, not proposed. It is open source, uses no paid service anywhere, and is trained only on freely available and openly licensed Indian Sign Language data. It will stay free.
